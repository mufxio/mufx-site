Evening Price Spikes in ERCOT: Net Load, Dispatchable Capacity and the Day-Ahead Premium

Working paper

Hao Zhang

Date2026-10-28
MarketERCOT · HB_HUBAVG
Data2019-01 – 2026-09
Statusworking paper
Abstract

This paper describes evening price spikes in the ERCOT real-time market using 15-minute settlement prices and generation by fuel from January 2019 to September 2026. Grid-tightness indicators (headroom and Physical Responsive Capability) are strongly associated with the contemporaneous price level but do not lead it. Changes coincide within the same 5-minute dispatch interval, and the tightest decile is followed by a higher price one hour later in only 34% of cases. Spike timing has shifted from 15:00–18:00 in 2019 to 19:00–22:00 in 2025, tracking sunset as solar capacity grew. Across 1,369 evenings since 2023, the probability of a $100/MWh interval rises from 8% when gas and battery fleets have spare output to 91% when both run near their trailing 30-day maximum. Low wind at sunset is the largest day-to-day source of variation. A simple test of buying sunset hours day-ahead and settling at real-time finds a consistently negative typical outcome. This is consistent with day-ahead prices already including a premium for scarcity risk.

1Data and definitions

  • Price. ERCOT real-time settlement point price, hub average (HB_HUBAVG), 15-minute intervals, Jan 2019 – Sep 2026. Day-ahead hourly prices for the same hub.
  • Generation. ERCOT settled generation by fuel, 15-minute intervals. Battery discharge is taken from the "Other" category (correlation 0.97 with EIA battery data, Nov 2024 – 2025).
  • Net load. Demand minus solar minus wind.
  • Fleet use. Gas (or battery) output at the evening net-load peak divided by that fleet's maximum output over the previous 30 days. Only past data is used.
  • Tightness. Headroom (online capacity minus dispatched output, a proxy built from the dispatch archive) and PRC, each below the 10th percentile of the trailing 90 days.
  • Evening and spike. Evening = 16:00–23:00 Central. A spike evening has at least one 15-minute price ≥ $100/MWh.

Each test's definitions and outcome measures were written down before results were computed. ERCOT's market redesign (RTC+B, 5 Dec 2025) changes how reserves are procured, so 2026 is reported separately where it matters.

2Tightness and price move together

Headroom, PRC and the price come out of the same security-constrained dispatch run. Their contemporaneous association is strong. Their predictive association is weak and has the opposite sign from the naive expectation, because tight intervals cluster at the daily price peak.

3Timing

Spike intervals concentrate in a three-hour window that has moved later each year as solar capacity grew (peak solar output: 2.0 GW in 2019, 29.9 GW in 2025). In 2024–2026, 72–74% of spike days had their spike inside the main evening window. The clock predicts timing but not occurrence: 36% of days in 2025 and 21% in Jan–Sep 2026 had any interval at or above $100/MWh.

4The net-load squeeze

Spikes occur when net load at the evening peak is high relative to the gas and battery output actually available that day. Figure 2 compares two August 2025 evenings with nearly identical peak demand (80.9 and 79.9 GW). On 20 August, wind at the net-load peak was 3.7 GW against 16.4 GW on 5 August. Net load peaked 10.6 GW higher, gas and battery fleets reached 99% and 98% of their trailing maxima (vs 80% and 86%), and the hub price reached $326/MWh (vs $49).

Ranked against the evening maximum price, wind at sunset has the largest correlation in recent years (−0.5 to −0.6). The level and size of the net-load climb matter. The top quintile of evening ramps spiked on 45–70% of evenings and the bottom quintile on 10–14%. The speed of the climb adds little. April and October, the main maintenance months, show the highest spike rates for large ramps (8 of 8 and 21 of 35 evenings). That pattern has not been checked against outage data.

Battery capacity has offset part of the rise in net load. The evening net-load ramp grew from 6.7 GW (2019) to 26.2 GW (2026). Over the same period, average battery discharge at the evening peak rose from near zero to 6.9 GW, and the share of evenings reaching $250/MWh fell from 21.6% in 2023 to 5.1% in 2026.

5Day-ahead versus real-time

The test buys 1 MW in the day-ahead market for each of three sunset hours, which settles against the real-time price. P&L per MWh = real-time hourly average − day-ahead price. Four day-selection rules use only information available before day-ahead bids are due. Rules were developed on 2019–2023 and checked on 2024. January 2025 – September 2026 is held out and has not been examined.

The typical day loses for every rule: the median is negative in all eight rows, and real time settles below day-ahead on 61–77% of days. After a spike, day-ahead averaged $189/MWh against a real-time outcome of $184. The day-ahead price includes a scarcity premium and tends to overshoot after recent spikes. The opposite position, selling day-ahead, has a positive median but large tail losses; 16 February 2021 alone costs about $7,900 per MW.

6Limitations

  • Historical headroom is a proxy (online high sustained limit minus base point). It matches the published 2026 method at r = 0.97.
  • The net-load results use realised values. A forecast version using ERCOT's day-ahead wind, solar, load and outage forecasts is specified but not run.
  • Trailing maximum output is not available capacity, and battery discharge is not state of charge.
  • Publication timing of dispatch data is assumed (about a 2-minute delay), not measured.
