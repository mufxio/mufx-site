Where Does the Marginal Cargo Go?

Europe's gas balance, US liquefaction limits and the HH–TTF–JKM link

Hao Zhang

Date2026-11-23
MarketsTTF · Henry Hub · JKM
Data2016 – 2026
Statusworking paper
Abstract

This paper follows one chain of reasoning: European gas demand, how Europe covers it, how much LNG Europe needs, how Europe and Asia compete for that LNG, and whether binding US liquefaction capacity limits the transmission of overseas prices to Henry Hub.

A monthly European supply balance built from public data (production + pipeline imports + LNG send-out + net storage withdrawal) comes out about 2.6% below Eurostat’s official consumption figure, close enough to work with. Within that balance, storage does almost all the work of following demand: consumption correlates 0.957 with net storage withdrawal and −0.006 with LNG send-out. LNG doesn’t follow the seasonal demand cycle, and EU-wide, import terminals never ran above 79% of capacity. On prices, Henry Hub is only weakly linked to TTF (weekly correlation 0.09–0.17), while same-month TTF and JKM move almost as one price (0.994). The liquefaction hypothesis points the expected way (HH–TTF correlation 0.05 in months with US plants at ≥ 90% utilisation vs 0.24 otherwise), but only 11 such months exist, too few for a meaningful test. Competition for cargoes is visible in quantities rather than prices: Europe’s share of US cargoes doubled in 2022, and most of the extra volume came from buyers without long-term contracts.

1The question

The research began with a chain of physical links, each a hypothesis:

  1. Europe’s demand is met by domestic production, pipeline imports, LNG and storage. Whatever production and pipelines don’t cover must come from LNG or storage.
  2. When Europe needs more LNG, it competes with Asia for flexible cargoes. That competition should show up in the TTF–JKM spread, in freight rates, and in where US cargoes go.
  3. US exporters supply most flexible cargoes. If US liquefaction plants are already full, a higher overseas price cannot draw more gas out of the US, so overseas price moves should transmit weakly to Henry Hub.

Each link was tested with public data. Section 2 covers link 1, Sections 3–4 link 2, and Section 5 link 3.

2Europe’s gas balance

2.1 The accounting identity

For the EU-27, monthly, January 2021 to August 2026:

supply = production (ex-Poland) + net external pipeline imports + LNG send-out + (storage withdrawals − injections)

Component Source
Production Eurostat monthly primary production
Pipeline imports ENTSOG physical flows at every EU border point, from a dated ledger of entry points (Nord Stream, Yamal, Ukraine transit, TurkStream, Baltic Pipe and others)
LNG send-out GIE ALSI
Storage GIE AGSI+
Check Eurostat calculated and observed inland consumption

Against Eurostat’s calculated consumption, the assembled balance runs a median 2.6% below over 48 months with complete pipeline data (range −7% to +1%). After aligning the treatment of Polish production, the median difference is −1.1%. The balance is a working measurement. Poland’s production has been confidential since October 2023, and 20 months have incomplete pipeline days.

Measured as year-on-year changes, the LNG correlation is −0.31, driven by 2022, when demand fell and LNG rose to replace Russian pipeline gas. Storage swings about 4.5 times as much as LNG and moves almost one-for-one with demand. LNG send-out is nearly flat by comparison: its seasonal swing is 8–16% of storage’s in every year. Storage follows the demand cycle; LNG fills the gap left by lost pipeline gas.

Defining residual need as consumption minus production minus pipeline imports, LNG plus storage matches it almost exactly (r = 0.9995, n = 47). In winter (November–March), LNG covered this share of residual need:

Period LNG share of winter residual need
2021 to Feb 2022 27%
Rest of 2022 66%
2023–2024 45%
2025–2026 48%

When pipeline supply changes, storage absorbs the difference (slope −1.52), not LNG (slope −0.135).

2.3 What does not pull LNG into Europe

Of 29 pre-stated hypotheses about Europe’s LNG intake, 12 failed or were contradicted:

  • Storage below normal → more LNG 1–2 weeks later. The within-period correlation changes sign: −0.15 before the 2022 invasion, then +0.52, +0.30 and +0.33.
  • Pipeline drop → more LNG two weeks later: r = +0.07 (n = 277).
  • Higher demand → more LNG: r = −0.006 (Table 1).
  • Terminal capacity binds when storage is tight: EU-wide utilisation never exceeded 79.25% on any day. Individual terminals weren’t tested; some in Northwest Europe were likely full in 2022.

One substitution does hold: year-on-year, lower pipeline supply goes with higher LNG at monthly frequency (r = −0.49; −0.84 in 2023–24, −0.67 in 2025–26). LNG replaces lost Russian pipeline gas over months, while storage handles weeks and seasons.

Neither TTF nor Henry Hub leads the other at weekly frequency (lagged correlations −0.08 to 0.12, none significant).

European delivered LNG is effectively TTF. The ACER assessment is TTF plus a small, usually negative benchmark, averaged over ten weekdays, so its weekly movements partly echo last week’s TTF. The link that matters runs through quantities, not weekly prices.

A similar pattern shows up in prices. TTF tends to be high when EU storage is below normal (−0.61), while LNG send-out barely relates to it (−0.09). Much of the storage link is one long swing, from the 2022 shortage to the 2023–24 surplus, so it says more about regimes than about week-to-week moves.

4Europe and Asia competing for cargoes

Same-month JKM and TTF are one market. At 0.994, the spread between them is small and slow-moving. Flexible cargoes arbitrage it away, so the competition is not visible as a widening price gap.

It is visible in three other places, each with caveats:

  • The Asia premium and storage. The winter JKM-over-TTF premium narrowed as EU storage fell below normal (levels r = 0.85). This fits Europe bidding up to attract cargoes when short, but the effective sample is about four independent observations.
  • Freight. Atlantic spot freight rises with the Asia-over-Europe premium, month by month (r ≈ 0.6–0.8 after removing outliers, n = 26). This fits cargoes going long-haul to Asia and tying up ships. It is same-month only, with no lead.
  • Where US cargoes go. Europe’s share of US cargoes went from 0.34 to 0.68 in 2022, fell from 0.69 to 0.57 during the 2023–24 Panama and Red Sea disruptions, and fell from 0.72 to 0.48 after the 2026 Hormuz disruption.

Figure 2 · Share of US LNG export volume by destination, monthly, January 2017 – July 2026. Asia = Northeast Asia plus other Asia. Source: DOE LNG export reports.

Who gives up cargoes. In 2022, Europe imported about 45 Mt more LNG (+44.7 Mt on GIIGNL figures), while world supply rose only about 20 Mt. Most of the difference came from buyers relying on flexible Atlantic cargoes without long-term contracts.

Europe and South Asia on GIIGNL figures; others from reconciled customs data. For China, lower demand (lockdowns, pipeline gas, coal) explains part of the drop. The figures mix sources, so they don’t sum exactly.

Exporters outside the US barely responded: output rose 1.4% in 2022 (2.8% on GIIGNL figures). In a shortage, the marginal cargo comes from the US or from another buyer giving one up.

Cargoes and knowable prices. Europe’s share of US cargoes tracks the lagged netback differential only through the post-2022 regime shift. Within each regime the slope is about zero, and month-to-month shares do not respond to netback changes knowable in advance (out-of-sample R² −0.04 to −0.07).

5The liquefaction hypothesis

If US plants are full, overseas prices should decouple from Henry Hub. The test splits months by US LNG export utilisation (exports ÷ peak capacity):

Table 4. Monthly HH–TTF correlation by US export utilisation, 2016–2026.

US utilisation Months HH–TTF correlation
≥ 90% 11 0.05
< 90% 115 0.24

The difference points the way the hypothesis predicts. But with 11 high-utilisation months, all in winter, the test had about an 11% chance of detecting an effect of plausible size. The result is uninformative. The hypothesis is neither supported nor refuted. Utilisation before 2021 also uses capacity figures that EIA revised later.

Two further checks give no clean answer:

  • US supply shocks have no identified directional effect on Henry Hub. Henry Hub fell 9.6% in the five days after 15 unplanned outages, but restarts and capacity additions were followed by a similar fall (−11.8%).
  • The 41% TTF rise around the June 2022 Freeport fire was mostly the Nord Stream cuts of the same days (37 of 41 log points on 14–15 June).

A proper test needs daily utilisation (scheduled feedgas from pipeline postings is now collectable) and more months of full plants. Golden Pass and Plaquemines will add capacity and change the denominator.

7Limitations

  • Gaps in the balance: daily demand and production are not published (Eurostat is monthly, 2–3 months late), Poland’s production is confidential from October 2023, and 20 months have incomplete pipeline data.
  • Few crises: the sample has one full scarcity regime (2021–22), plus the 2026 Hormuz disruption, which is still unfolding and wasn’t analysed in depth. Storage–price relationships are hard to separate from those episodes.
  • JKM data: free JKM data is limited to ICE deferred contracts from October 2024. Platts JKM and expired contracts are paid.
  • Cargo destinations: DOE records destination at departure. Diversions, resales and contract terms are not public.
  • Timing: most results are same-period associations. Where publication delays were respected, no physical fundamental led prices (72 monthly tests, none significant after correction).
